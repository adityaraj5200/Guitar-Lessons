# Lesson 3 — Chord Diagrams, Am & Changing Chords

By the end of this lesson, you should understand a chord diagram, know **Em and Am**, and begin switching between them.

## 1. How to read a chord diagram

You'll constantly encounter diagrams like this:

```text
       Am

       X  0  2  2  1  0
       E  A  D  G  B  e

          ┌───┬───┬───
          │   │   │
          │   │ ● │
          │ ● │ ● │
          │   │   │
```

Different websites/apps draw them slightly differently, so let's understand the underlying idea.



The **vertical lines** represent your six strings.

The **horizontal lines** represent frets.

A black dot tells you:

> **Press this string at this fret.**

At the top you'll usually see either:

**O** = play that string open  
**X** = don't play that string

Finger numbers are:

```text
1 = Index
2 = Middle
3 = Ring
4 = Pinky
```

That's essentially all you need to decode thousands of chord diagrams.

---

# 2. Your second chord — A minor (Am)

You already know:

**Em = E minor**

Now we're learning:

**Am = A minor**

The fingering is:

```text
6th E ─── X ───  Don't play
5th A ─── 0 ───  Open
4th D ─── 2 ───  Middle finger
3rd G ─── 2 ───  Ring finger
2nd B ─── 1 ───  Index finger
1st e ─── 0 ───  Open
```

Or, compactly:

**X 0 2 2 1 0**

### Put your fingers down

**Index (1)** → 2nd string, fret 1  
**Middle (2)** → 4th string, fret 2  
**Ring (3)** → 3rd string, fret 2

Notice something interesting.

Your middle and ring fingers are both on **fret 2**, but on neighbouring strings.

Make sure each finger is curved so it doesn't accidentally touch another string.

---

# 3. Test Am properly

Don't immediately strum.

While holding Am, individually pluck:

```text
5 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1
```

We're deliberately starting at string 5 because string 6 isn't part of the basic Am shape.

You want five clean sounds.

If, for example, your 1st string sounds like:

> **thud**

your ring finger may be accidentally touching it.

Adjust your fingers until all five strings ring.

Then:

**↓ STRUM**

Congratulations—you now know two chords.

---

# 4. Why aren't we playing the 6th string?

This introduces an important concept.

The notes in an A minor chord are:

**A + C + E**

Interestingly, your open 6th string is **E**, so technically it belongs to Am.

But beginners are generally taught the standard open Am voicing starting from the **A string**, giving A as the lowest/root note and producing the familiar Am sound.

So for now:

> When playing Am, start your strum from string **5**.

You'll eventually understand exactly why when we study chord construction.

---

# 5. Your first chord change

Now comes one of the most important skills you'll practice over the next few weeks.

Play:

**Em → Am → Em → Am**

Don't strum yet.

Form Em:

```text
0 2 2 0 0 0
```

Hold it.

Then move to Am:

```text
X 0 2 2 1 0
```

Then back to Em.

Your brain is currently learning:

> "Where do these fingers need to go without me consciously positioning each one?"

That's called **muscle memory**.

At first:

**Em → stare at fretboard → move one finger → move another → fix finger → Am**

That's completely fine.

Eventually:

**Em → Am**

will happen almost instantly.

---

# 6. Don't lift your fingers unnecessarily high

This is a very common beginner problem.

You might currently do:

```text
Em

      fingers
         ↑
         ↑
         ↑
         ↑
         ↑

Am
```

Your fingers fly several centimetres away from the fretboard before finding the next chord.

Try keeping them **close to the strings** during transitions.

Smaller movement = faster chord changes.

You don't need speed now, but developing efficient movement early helps enormously.

---

# 7. Now introduce rhythm

Once you can form both chords, we're going to combine them with the rhythm from Lesson 2.

Play:

```text
Em
↓   ↓   ↓   ↓
1   2   3   4

Am
↓   ↓   ↓   ↓
1   2   3   4

Em
↓   ↓   ↓   ↓
1   2   3   4

Am
↓   ↓   ↓   ↓
1   2   3   4
```

**Do not stop the counting during the chord change.**

That's difficult!

Initially you might get:

> 1...2...3...4... **[five-second struggle]** ...1...2...

That's okay.

Prioritize making the chord correctly first. Speed comes later.

---

# 8. A powerful chord-change exercise

Set a **60 BPM metronome**.

You'll hear:

> tick... tick... tick... tick...

Each tick represents one beat.

Play one downward strum per tick:

**↓ ↓ ↓ ↓**

Four beats of Em.

Then four beats of Am.

Eventually:

```text
| Em            | Am            |
| ↓  ↓  ↓  ↓    | ↓  ↓  ↓  ↓   |
| 1  2  3  4    | 1  2  3  4   |
```

This is your first real **chord progression**.

---

# 9. What is a chord progression?

It's simply:

> **A sequence of chords played in a particular order.**

So:

**Em → Am → Em → Am**

is a chord progression.

Songs aren't usually one chord played forever. Their harmony changes as the song progresses.

Soon you'll learn progressions such as:

**G → D → Em → C**

That's especially useful because countless pop/Bollywood songs use progressions involving these chords.

And it gets us closer to your goal of playing **Kabhi Jo Baadal Barse**.

## Your Lesson 3 practice 🎯

Don't rush into Lesson 4 immediately. Spend a few sessions getting this comfortable.

Practice for about **20 minutes**:

**5 min:** Form Am → release → form Am → release.

**5 min:** Individually test all five Am strings until they're consistently clean.

**5 min:** Practice **Em ↔ Am** without strumming.

**5 min:** Play:

```text
Em   ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓
Am   ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓
Em   ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓
Am   ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓
```

Your immediate goal isn't speed. It's getting **clean notes with the minimum finger pressure necessary**, especially given the pressure issue you noticed earlier.

Once you can make Em and Am reasonably reliably, **Lesson 4 will introduce your first major chords (C and G), explain what "major vs minor" actually means, and start making your practice sound much more like real songs.**
